Prompts matter, but an agent that can take actions needs something more basic: permission boundaries. A chatbot that only drafts text creates one kind of risk. An agent that can send messages, update records, approve expenses, or call external systems creates a much larger operational risk because an incorrect answer can become an incorrect action.</p><p>The first design question should therefore be what the agent is allowed to do without human approval. Some actions may be reversible and low risk, such as creating a draft calendar event or summarizing an internal document. Other actions may affect money, customers, legal commitments, or security. A useful design separates read access, draft access, execution access, and high-risk execution rather than giving one broad permission.</p><p>The second issue is thresholds. Human organizations already use approval limits for expenses, contracts, discounts, and access rights. AI agents can use similar rules. An agent might be allowed to prepare a refund under a certain amount but require a person to approve larger refunds. It might update a non-sensitive field automatically while requesting confirmation before changing an account owner or payment instruction. Thresholds turn vague ideas about “human oversight” into concrete operating rules.</p><p>Logging is equally important. When an agent takes an action, the organization should be able to reconstruct what information it used, what instruction it received, what tool it called, and what result came back. Without this record, incident investigation becomes difficult. Good logs are not only for technical debugging; they support audit, user complaints, security review, and process improvement.</p><p>Agents also need clear escalation paths. If the agent encounters missing information, conflicting instructions, or an unusual request, it should know when to stop.
